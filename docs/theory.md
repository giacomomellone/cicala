# Theory

[Design](design.md) governs the product. This page governs the question
corpus: what a good question asks a person to do, how to revise one, and where
depth becomes pressure. The [English style
guide](https://github.com/giacomomellone/kveld/blob/main/questions/en/STYLE.md)
and [German style
guide](https://github.com/giacomomellone/kveld/blob/main/questions/de/STYLE.md)
turn this theory into language-specific rules.

## Retrieval and construction

A boring question is answered by retrieval. The answer already exists, fully
formed, and the person only has to recite it. “What do you do for work?” usually
retrieves a short introduction used many times before. Reciting it costs little
and reveals little.

An interesting question forces construction. The person must search memory,
notice a detail, make a comparison, or choose between competing answers, then
build a response in the room. “Which part of your work would surprise your
12-year-old self?” uses familiar material, but the answer is unlikely to exist
in finished form. The construction is where a person reveals how they notice
and interpret their life.

This gives writers and reviewers a useful test: imagine a typical person
hearing the question. If a rehearsed answer arrives intact, the question has
failed. Open-ended grammar is not enough. “What are your hobbies?” is
open-ended and still retrieves a profile. “Which hobby has changed how you
notice ordinary things?” requires construction.

Construction also has a cost. A question can demand memory, judgment, exposure,
or all three. Depth is the amount of that cost, not the seriousness of the
topic. “What did you eat after school as a child?” and “Which childhood food
were you embarrassed to admit you liked?” share a topic; the second asks for
more exposure.

## The information gap

[George Loewenstein’s information-gap
account](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.116.1.75) describes curiosity as the
feeling produced when attention lands on a gap between what someone knows and
what they want to know. The gap needs a reachable edge. If it is too small,
“What colour is snow?” is already settled. If it is too large, “What is life
about?” offers no place to start. Research on interest likewise finds that
novelty and complexity work together with a person’s sense that the material is
understandable, rather than rewarding obscurity by itself ([Silvia,
2005](https://doi.org/10.1037/1528-3542.5.1.89)).

Constraint makes a useful gap. “What do you think about music?” leaves the
whole subject open and gives attention nowhere to land. “Which song do you love
for a reason that has nothing to do with the music?” closes most routes and
opens one answer-shaped gap. Openness means room for different answers;
unbounded openness destroys the gap.

## Expectation violation

Interest needs the possibility of surprise. For corpus work, treat that as an
answer-distribution test: imagine ten people answering. “What’s your favourite
colour?” produces a narrow distribution even though nobody can predict each
answer exactly. “What’s an opinion your friends would disagree with?” is built
around a violated expectation and can travel in many directions.

If eight imagined answers cluster, add a constraint that excludes the obvious
answer or discard the question. “What food do you dislike?” may cluster around
a few familiar dislikes. “Which food do you wish you liked, and what keeps
stopping you?” adds conflict and makes the explanation harder to retrieve.
Wide distribution is an editorial test derived from the role of novelty in
interest; it is not a published scoring formula.

## Concreteness over abstraction

Abstract questions invite defended positions. “Do you value honesty?” asks for
a principle, and “yes” is socially correct before the conversation begins.
Concrete questions invite scenes: “When did you last tell a lie you’re still
unsure about?” supplies an event, an action, and unresolved judgment. A scene
gives the listener people, objects, and choices to ask about. It also shows a
value in use rather than asking the speaker to advertise one.

Concrete does not mean trivial. “Which chair did you sit in when your family
argued?” can carry more depth than “How did conflict shape you?” The first
question lets the person decide how much meaning to attach to a physical detail;
the second demands an essay about the self.

## Presupposition

Every question smuggles in assumptions. Use that fact generously. “What went
well this year?” assumes that something did and directs attention toward
evidence for it. “Who supported you this year?” assumes support and can make a
specific relationship easier to notice.

A presupposition should offer a foothold, not invent someone’s biography.
“Which parent understands you better?” assumes two parents, a relationship with
both, and a ranking. “Who understood you better than you expected?” keeps the
useful assumption—that someone did—without prescribing who. The same safety
test applies to work, romance, health, money, family, and childhood.

## Hypotheticals as freedom

A hypothetical lets someone try a position without claiming it as a plan or a
confession. “If pay and status stayed the same, which part of your work would
you stop doing?” separates the work itself from two common reasons for keeping
it. The answerer can explore dissatisfaction without announcing that they
intend to quit.

Good hypotheticals isolate one variable. “If you could change anything about
society, what would it be?” is abstraction with a costume. “If short trips had
to be car-free, what would change first in your week?” fixes the rule and asks
for a concrete consequence. The imagined world creates freedom; the constraint
creates the question.

## The transform toolkit

Start with “What’s a smell that reminds you of your childhood?” It is already
half-decent. Smell is sensory and often involuntary, so the question can bypass
rehearsed self-presentation and produce a scene. Its weakness is that many
people have a cached answer: a grandparent’s cooking, a school corridor, cut
grass. The answer distribution is narrower than it first appears.

- **Add a constraint:** “What’s a childhood smell you’ve never been able to
  find again?” Scarcity makes the person search and explain what is missing. It
  also introduces loss: the source may be a dead relative, a lost home, or a
  childhood the person does not want to revisit.

- **Ask for change:** “What’s a smell you loved as a kid and now can’t stand?”
  The contrast requires two versions of the self and a story about the turn. It
  excludes people whose response never changed and can steer a warm memory
  toward disgust or disappointment.

- **Invert or negate:** “What’s a smell everyone finds gross but takes you
  back?” The social mismatch widens the answer distribution and makes the
  answer revealing. It also asks the person to expose an embarrassing taste
  and gives the table material that could be mocked.

- **Force selection:** “If your childhood were one smell, which moment made you
  pick it?” Selection makes the answerer choose a representative detail; the
  second clause asks for evidence. “One” can feel like an exam, especially for
  someone with scattered memories or several childhood homes.

- **Go second-order:** “Whose house had a smell you’d know instantly?” Moving
  from the smell to its owner opens a relationship and a place. That person may
  be dead, estranged, or associated with an unsafe home, so the relational gain
  comes with a higher chance of grief.

No transform improves every question. Each excludes easy answers by narrowing
the route, and each exclusion can also remove a safe answer. Review the gain in
construction and the cost in exposure together. A sharper question is not
automatically a better question for every depth.

## Failure mode: shame

A construction question asks the person to produce something in public. When
nothing comes, they may conclude that they are empty or boring rather than that
the question was difficult. “What’s the best book you’ve ever read?” is
especially bad: it is both a memory exam and a public ranking of taste.

Lower the performance demand without removing construction. The asker can
answer first and answer imperfectly: “Mine is a book I loved at sixteen and
probably wouldn’t finish now.” Ask for observation instead of a verdict:
“Which book do you still think about at odd moments?” Replace the superlative
with the recent: “Which book did you last recommend?” Move into a domain where
the answerer is the expert: “Which story did you ask to hear again as a child?”
Permit slowness by leaving silence after “Which book changed for you when you
reread it?”

The deepest fix belongs to the asker. Interestingness is produced by attention,
not by impressive answer content. If someone answers “coffee” to “What did you
look forward to this week?”, “Where did you drink it?” can uncover the place,
ritual, or person around it. A flat answer is raw material, not a dead end.

## Failure mode: the exhausted archive

Long-term partners have a different problem. A retrieval question such as
“Where did you grow up?” has been answered, followed up, and retold. Making it
more intimate may only mine the same archive harder.

Familiarity also creates overconfidence. Swann and Gill found that relationship
length and involvement increased confidence in impressions of partners without
consistently increasing accuracy about matters such as preferences and sexual
history ([1997](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.73.4.747)). In experiments on
the [closeness-communication
bias](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2010.09.005), friends and spouses
overestimated how successfully they communicated ambiguous meanings more than
strangers did. The practical danger is a closed loop: “I know what you think”
replaces the question, so no new answer can correct the assumption.

Change the tense before increasing the depth. Ask “What are you looking forward
to next month?” instead of requesting another childhood story. Ask about drift:
“What’s something you used to want and don’t now?” Compare interpretations
rather than facts: “Which part of our first trip do you think we remember
differently?” Go second-order: “What do you think I still misunderstand about
your work?”

A new environment can supply material that neither person owns yet. In three
experiments, couples doing a shared novel and arousing task showed a greater
short-term increase in experienced relationship quality than couples doing a
more mundane task ([Aron et al.,
2000](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.78.2.273)). This does not show that any
novel outing repairs a relationship. It does support giving partners a third
object to react to instead of demanding that they generate novelty from each
other. That is the corpus reason for the `here` category: “Which object in this
room would be hardest to explain to someone from 200 years ago?”

## What the research does and does not support

Research supports disclosure and responsive attention, with limits. Collins
and Miller’s [1994
meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.116.3.457) found three
relationships: intimate disclosers tended to be liked more, people disclosed
more to those they already liked, and people tended to like someone more after
disclosing to them. It did not show that maximum disclosure is always better.
In an experiment with strangers, immediate turn-taking disclosure produced
more liking, closeness, perceived similarity, and enjoyment than one person
disclosing while the other listened before they switched roles ([Sprecher et
al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2013.03.017)).

Aron and colleagues tested a 45-minute procedure of reciprocal tasks that
escalated in intensity. In its first study, it produced more post-interaction
closeness than comparable small-talk tasks ([1997](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167297234003)).
That is evidence for structured, reciprocal disclosure in a particular setting,
not proof that a deck can manufacture lasting intimacy.

Question-asking research points to responsiveness after the first answer.
Across two experiments and an observational speed-dating study, people who
asked more questions, especially follow-up questions, were better liked; in the
speed-dating data, more follow-up questions were associated with a greater
chance of being offered a second date ([Huang et al.,
2017](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspi0000097)). The field result is an association,
not a ranking of every conversational move.

People also underestimate deeper conversation. Experiments with strangers
found that participants expected others to care less than they did, expected
more awkwardness than they experienced, and after trying both shallow and deep
conversations preferred the deeper one more than they had predicted ([Kardas,
Kumar, and Epley,
2022](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspa0000281)). Participants also described their
typical conversations with strangers as less intimate than they would ideally
prefer. This supports making substantive questions available. It does not
justify forcing them or assuming that every table wants the same depth.

Research does not supply Kveld's deck taxonomy. Decks are UI affordances,
not empirical kinds of conversation. Corpus-linguistic work on 2.3 million
words of conversation classified stretches of talk by communicative purposes
and found 16 discourse types; conventional genres such as jokes and personal
narratives were rare ([Biber et al.,
2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pragma.2020.09.018)). Most talk would be called
“chat” at the product level even though it contains local purposes such as
evaluating, explaining, planning, and joking.

Five current decks name a relationship or shared place: New People, Close,
Family, Work, and Here. They change what the question may assume:

- **New People** assumes no shared history. It should not test recall about the
  relationship or ask for a biography on demand.
- **Close** assumes familiarity. Basic archive questions are often exhausted,
  so it asks about current change, interpretation, and things that may not have
  been said yet.
- **Family** permits shared history but keeps the whole prompt safe and
  answerable for a 10-year-old.
- **Work** recognizes a setting with status and consequences. A question that
  is acceptable among friends may be coercive between colleagues.
- **Here** uses the room, table, event, or visible surroundings as a third
  object. Joint attention lets a group begin without making one person the
  immediate object of disclosure.

Wild is the deliberate exception to the relationship-or-place naming rule. It
is an explicit opt-in to dark, spicy, macabre, or absurd tone. `random` would
describe the sampling mechanism and `anything` would conceal the tone change.
Wild is not evidence of depth and does not mean the heaviest available
question.

One question may be eligible for several decks. “What did you stop enjoying
when it became work?” can fit Close and Work without being copied or assigned
an objective category. Dark and spicy questions are Wild-only because
eligibility overlap must not leak their tone into Family or Work.

## Consequences for Kveld

Depth remains editorial exposure-cost metadata, independent from tone. The
first physical prototype has no depth control. Normal playback samples depths 1
and 2 from the selected deck; depth 3 remains browseable and reviewable but is
not served automatically.

This is a consent decision under uncertainty, not a claim that shallow talk is
better. A public slider might let someone set a boundary without speaking, but
moving a date or work table toward “light” can also become a public verdict on
the people present. That interaction needs observation before it earns a
physical part. Until then, Next is the low-cost rejection mechanism.

There is still no ramp. The device does not infer that the table is “ready”
after several questions, and it has no reliable session boundary. “Which habit
have you changed this month?” must stand on its own instead of being unlocked
by answering “What made you laugh today?”

Texture is not a ramp either. The bag prefers a different depth band and form
than the question just shown, which decorrelates consecutive draws; it claims
nothing about the table's readiness and never raises depth over time.

Reciprocity is a hard style rule: never write a question the asker could not
also answer. “Which mistake do people like you keep making?” points outward and
judges. “Which mistake do you keep repeating?” can pass around the table.

Follow-up belongs in human attention. Hardware cannot know whether “my
brother’s kitchen” calls for “What was cooking?” or “Do you still see him?” A
useful built-in second clause may live in the question text—“Which rule did you
break, and what happened?”—but the device should not display a generic
interrogation after the answer.

The 95-character editorial ceiling is an asset because it forces the writer to
choose the gap and leave the conversation to the table. The schema’s
140-character technical guard is not permission to add setup, disclaimers, and
three prompts. “Which promise became harder to keep than to break?” has one
job.

Superlatives deserve a validator review flag because `best`, `worst`, `most`,
and `least` often turn construction into an exam. The flag should prompt a
rewrite such as “What made you laugh hardest this week?” to “What made you
laugh this week?”, not reject every earned use automatically.
